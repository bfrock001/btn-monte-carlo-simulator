      Transparency

      How this tool works

      Every number here comes from the same idea: replay a retirement thousands of times against real historical markets, and see how often the money lasts. This page lays out exactly how that’s done — the data, the engine, the strategies, the optimizer — and, just as important, what it does not model. Nothing here is a black box.

      1. What this is — and isn’t

      This is a bootstrap Monte Carlo retirement simulator with a portfolio optimizer bolted on. It runs entirely in your browser — no data leaves your device, and there is no account or server. It is an educational tool for exploring how spending, allocation, and market history interact. It is not financial advice, not a prediction, and not tuned to your personal situation, taxes, or goals.

      2. The data

      Annual total returns and U.S. inflation come from the Bogleheads’ Simba Backtesting Spreadsheet, a long-running community data set of asset-class returns. The tool covers 22 asset classes (US equity by size and style, international developed and emerging, Treasuries by maturity, TIPS, corporates, T-bills, REITs, and gold).

      • Coverage varies by asset. Some series start later than others (emerging markets, TIPS). Where a portfolio holds an asset that has no data in a given year, that year is skipped rather than guessed — the tool tells you when that happens.
      • Some early history is reconstructed. A few series use proxy/spliced data before their real inception (e.g. TIPS before 1997 is treated as unavailable, not invented).
      • You choose the window: full data set, post-WWII (1946+), modern era (1972+, the default), or a custom range.

      3. The engine — one simulated retirement

      To simulate a single retirement over an N-year horizon, the engine does this:

      1. Draw the years. For each year of the horizon it picks one real historical calendar year at random, with replacement (a “bootstrap”). A 30-year retirement is 30 independently drawn historical years.
      2. Keep each year whole. A drawn year carries all of its asset returns and its inflation together, so the real relationships within a year — how stocks, bonds, and inflation moved relative to each other — are preserved.
      3. Blend and rebalance. That year’s portfolio return is the weighted mix of your allocation, rebalanced back to your target weights every year.
      4. Spend, then grow. Guaranteed income is netted against your spending; the remainder is withdrawn from the portfolio; what’s left earns the drawn year’s return.
      5. Repeat and tally. Do this for all N years, then run the whole thing thousands of times (1,000–10,000, your choice). “Success” means the portfolio never hit zero before the horizon ended.

      4. Real dollars & inflation

      Everything you enter and everything you see is in today’s dollars. Each drawn year carries its actual inflation, and spending is inflated by that bootstrapped inflation path so purchasing power stays constant. Ending values and growth rates are then deflated back to real terms, so a “$1M ending value” means $1M of today’s buying power.

      5. Guaranteed income

      Social Security, a pension, and an annuity can each be added and are netted against spending each year (any surplus is added back to the portfolio):

      • Social Security always receives an inflation COLA. You can model two earners (Spouse A and Spouse B), each with their own claim age. Spouse B has their own current age, so their benefits start on their timeline.
      • Pension — per spouse, with an optional COLA.
      • Annuity — with a start age and an optional stop age, so you can model an annuity that only bridges income until Social Security begins.

      6. Spending strategies

      How much you withdraw each year is driven by the strategy you pick on the Simulator tab:

      • Fixed expense schedule — spend your planned amount, inflation-adjusted, every year.
      • Constant dollar — the classic “4% rule” shape: year one’s amount, inflated annually thereafter.
      • Forgo inflation in down years — skip the inflation raise after a losing year; the skipped raise stays invested.
      • Actual spending decline — let real spending drift down over time, matching how retirees actually spend less as they age.
      • Guyton-Klinger guardrails — cut spending after bad markets, raise it after good ones, within upper/lower guardrails.
      • Vanguard dynamic spending — a ceiling-and-floor rule that caps how much the withdrawal can rise or fall year to year.

      7. Sequence-of-returns stress test

      Optionally, each simulation can be forced to take its worst historical year first (or 2008 specifically). Front-loading a crash is the hardest test of a plan, because a big loss early — while withdrawals continue — does the most lasting damage. This is off by default.

      8. What the numbers mean

      • Chance of success — the share of simulated retirements that never ran out of money.
      • Real median CAGR — the middle simulation’s annualized real growth rate.
      • Median ending value (real) — the middle simulation’s leftover balance, in today’s dollars.
      • Max drawdown — the deepest peak-to-trough drop of the investments themselves (ignoring withdrawals): how severe a bear market the mix would have ridden through. Reported as the median across simulations.

      Medians are used rather than averages because a few extreme lucky runs can pull an average far from a typical outcome. Every figure is a Monte-Carlo estimate and carries sampling error.

      9. The Portfolio Optimizer

      The optimizer answers the inverse of the Simulator: which mix gives the most growth while keeping my chance of success at or above a floor? It holds your entire Simulator plan fixed — balance, horizon, spending, income, strategy, period — and varies only the allocation, scoring every candidate through the same engine above.

      • Two ways to search. Optimize Freely sweeps a constrained grid of your chosen asset classes (with per-asset min/max limits and a weight step). Two-step first finds the best stock/bond split, then refines the sub-classes inside that locked split.
      • The winner is the mix with the highest real median growth that still clears your success floor and an optional max-drawdown cap.
      • Fair comparison. Only assets with full data over your plan period enter the search, so every mix is judged over the same window.
      • Common Random Numbers. Every candidate mix is scored on the same set of bootstrapped return sequences. That way the difference between two mixes reflects the allocation change — not which random retirements each happened to draw — so the comparison curves are smooth and meaningful instead of jagged with sampling noise.

      10. Data & Correlations

      The reference tab shows the raw material behind the model:

      • Reference stats — each asset’s historical CAGR and volatility over your window.
      • Correlation matrix — Pearson correlation of annual returns over each pair’s common years (minimum 10 overlapping years).
      • Periodic table of returns — a Callan-style year-by-year ranking; you can overlay your loaded portfolio to see how the blend placed each calendar year.

      11. Assumptions & limitations

      Read this part. A model is only as honest as the caveats it admits:

      • History is not destiny. The future can look unlike any window in the data.
      • Single-year resampling understates sequence-of-returns risk. Because each year is drawn independently, the tool breaks up the real-world clustering of good and bad years — a 2000–2002 or 2007–2009 run rarely survives intact. It captures the range of one-year outcomes well, but sustained multi-year bear markets can run longer than the reshuffle implies. (The sequence-of-returns stress test is a partial answer.)
      • No taxes, fees, or costs. No income or capital-gains tax, fund expense ratios, advisor fees, or trading costs are modeled.
      • No account-type or RMD modeling. Tax-deferred, Roth, and taxable dollars are all treated the same; required minimum distributions are not simulated.
      • Frictionless rebalancing. The portfolio is rebalanced exactly to target every year, for free.
      • Fixed horizon, no mortality. The plan runs the full period you set. For couples, both income streams run the whole horizon — there is no survivor-benefit transition when one spouse would pass.
      • Estimates, not answers. Every figure is a Monte-Carlo estimate; it carries sampling error and will shift slightly each time you run.
